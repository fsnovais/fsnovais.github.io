Investing looks like a movie scene, brokers shouting on a trading floor. It is not. In
practice it is building a simple system and letting it run: money in, an allocation rule,
a periodic review. The hard part is not picking the asset, it is the order of the steps.

This is the script I follow, adapted to what exists in Brazil. It is not investment
advice, it is the decision structure. The tax figures reflect the rules in force today and
they change often, so check before acting.

First, a distinction that saves money: **investing is not trading**. Trading is buying low
and selling high over short horizons, and it is where people lose. Investing is growing
wealth consistently over years. The second one is boring, which is exactly why it works.

## Step 0: expensive debt beats any investment

If you carry credit card revolving debt or an overdraft, stop here. No investment
available to a retail investor pays what those debts charge. Revolving card debt in Brazil
runs into the hundreds of percent per year; the law now caps total interest and charges at
the original amount of the debt, which still means paying double.

Clearing that debt is an investment with a guaranteed, tax-free return. No CDB comes
close. A mortgage or a payroll loan is a different conversation, because the rate is low
and the horizon is long.

## Step 1: the emergency fund

This is the part most guides treat as a footnote and that actually holds up everything
else. Without a fund, the first surprise forces you to sell investments at the worst
possible moment, and the whole plan turns into a loss.

**How much.** Count in months of living costs, not months of salary. Add up what leaves
your account every month and cannot be cut: housing, food, transport, school, health plan,
instalments.

- salaried job, stable employer: 3 to 6 months
- contractor, self-employed, commission-based or variable income: 9 to 12 months
- single income household, small child, or an unstable sector: push it up

Living costs of R$ 5,000 a month on a contractor arrangement means a fund between
R$ 45,000 and R$ 60,000. That sounds like a lot, and it is. It gets built over a year or
two, and the first milestone can be a single month.

**Where.** Three requirements, in this order: same-day or next-day redemption, low credit
risk, and no chance the value is down on the day you need it. That leaves few sensible
options:

- Tesouro Selic, the government bond that tracks the base rate, with daily liquidity
- a daily-liquidity CDB paying close to 100% of the CDI, at a bank covered by the FGC
- a plain fixed income fund with a zero management fee and same-day redemption

The FGC deposit guarantee covers up to R$ 250,000 per taxpayer at each institution, capped
at R$ 1 million every four years. A large fund at one small bank with an attractive rate is
the moment to split across institutions.

**Where not.** Stocks, REITs, crypto, a two-year CDB, pension plans and property are not an
emergency fund. All of them can be down in exactly the month you lose your income. The
savings account is not ideal either: it pays less than Tesouro Selic and credits interest
only on the monthly anniversary, so withdrawing on day 20 throws the month away.

The fund does not exist to earn a return. It exists so you never have to sell anything in
a hurry. It is insurance, not investment.

## Step 2: a goal with a number and a deadline

Investing with no goal is playing football with no goalposts. "Have more money" is not a
goal; "put together R$ 300,000 in ten years for a property down payment" is.

For retirement, the quick calculation is to divide the income you want per year by a
conservative real withdrawal rate, between 3.5% and 4% a year. Wanting R$ 5,000 a month,
or R$ 60,000 a year, means something between R$ 1.5 million and R$ 1.7 million
accumulated, in today's money. The number is scary, and it is what turns a monthly
contribution into a decision instead of a guess.

With the target and the horizon in hand you can work out the contribution you need and
find out early whether the goal is unrealistic. Better now than in fifteen years.

## Step 3: the horizon picks the product

Before looking at returns, answer when you will need the money. The horizon rules out half
the options on its own.

- **under 2 years**: floating-rate fixed income with liquidity. There is no time to
  recover from a drop
- **2 to 5 years**: fixed income maturing when you need it, either prefixed or
  inflation-linked
- **over 10 years**: this is where equities make sense, because time absorbs the shocks

Risk profile questionnaires help, but the horizon of the money matters more than your
declared tolerance for risk. Almost everyone discovers they are conservative during their
first 30% drawdown.

## Step 4: the Brazilian menu

**Tesouro Direto.** Government bonds, the lowest credit risk in the country. Selic tracks
the base rate and works for the emergency fund and short horizons. IPCA+ pays inflation
plus a real rate and protects purchasing power over the long run, but swings in price if
you sell before maturity. Prefixado locks a rate and bets that rates will fall.

**CDB, LCI and LCA.** Lending to a bank, covered by the FGC. A CDB is usually quoted as a
percentage of the CDI. LCI and LCA pay less on paper but are exempt from income tax for
individuals, so they have to be compared after tax. They carry lock-up periods, which
rules them out of the emergency fund.

**Debêntures, CRI and CRA.** Corporate debt. The incentivized ones are tax-exempt, pay
more, and carry no FGC guarantee. The credit risk is yours.

**Funds.** Someone manages the money for you, charging a management fee and sometimes a
performance fee. Fixed income and multi-strategy funds suffer come-cotas, a tax
prepayment in May and November that eats into compounding. Equity funds do not.

**ETFs.** An exchange-traded fund that replicates an index, with a low fee because no
manager is picking anything. It is the simplest way to buy the whole market instead of
trying to pick the right stock.

**REITs (fundos imobiliários).** They own malls, warehouses, offices or real estate credit
and distribute rent monthly. The monthly distributions are income tax exempt for
individuals when the fund meets the legal conditions, which is why they are so popular
here.

**Stocks.** A piece of a company. The best expected long-run return, and the highest odds
of you doing something silly along the way.

**Private pension.** A PGBL lets you deduct contributions up to 12% of gross taxable
income if you file the long form, and taxes the full amount on withdrawal. A VGBL has no
deduction and taxes only the gains. It is worth it for the long-horizon regressive tax
table and for estate planning, not for the returns of most of these funds.

Rule of thumb: do not concentrate everything in one asset type. A portfolio of nothing but
REITs is as unbalanced as one of nothing but prefixed bonds.

## Step 5: how much the government and the broker eat

Gross returns do not pay bills. What matters is what is left.

- **fixed income and funds**: the regressive income tax table, 22.5% up to 180 days, 20%
  up to 360, 17.5% up to 720 and 15% beyond that. Staying invested is literally a discount
- **IOF**: charged on gains withdrawn within the first 30 days, gone after that
- **exempt for individuals**: LCI, LCA, the savings account, incentivized debentures, and
  monthly REIT distributions within the legal conditions
- **stocks**: 15% on gains from ordinary trades, exempt when your sales in the month stay
  under R$ 20,000, and 20% on day trades with no exemption
- **stock dividends**: still exempt in the hands of the receiver, with the new 10%
  withholding reaching only large payments, above R$ 50,000 a month from the same payer,
  under Law 15,270 of 2025
- **B3 custody fee**: 0.20% a year on Treasury bonds, waived on balances up to R$ 10,000
  in Tesouro Selic

None of this is stable. MP 1,303, which proposed a single 17.5% rate and the end of the
LCI and LCA exemption, expired in October 2025 without a vote, and the old rules stayed. So
confirm the current rule before making a decision driven by tax.

## Step 6: build it, automate it, review it

Set ranges instead of exact numbers. A young investor starting out usually sits around 70%
to 80% in equities with the rest in fixed income; close to spending the money, that flips.
What matters is having the range written down before the first drop, when deciding gets
hard.

Then automate the contribution. A scheduled transfer for the day after payday beats
discipline, because it takes the decision out of the way.

Review twice a year, not twice a week. If one class has drifted well past its range, sell
some and rebalance. Rebalancing feels wrong, you are selling exactly what went up, but it
is what keeps risk at the size you agreed to.

## The most expensive mistake

Almost everyone starts with the wrong question, which is "what should I buy". The order
that works is: expensive debt, emergency fund, goal, horizon, product. The first four steps
do not depend on getting any forecast right, and they decide most of the outcome.

The rest is time. Steady contributions and compounding do, in twenty years, work that no
clever stock pick does in twenty months.
